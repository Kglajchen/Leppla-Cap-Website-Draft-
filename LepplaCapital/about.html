A True Fiduciary, Not a Brokerage

How a review of unsuitable, illiquid fund holdings became the founding story of Leppla Capital and the structures we still use today.

The introduction

In 2017, Bruce Leppla was introduced to the leadership of a California ironworkers district council. The concern was simple. The council fund and related local funds were getting investment advice that did not look right.

The problem

The advisers at the time sat in brokerage offices, not fiduciary ones. The funds had been sold investments they did not fully understand. On review, those holdings were unsuitable: illiquid, high-fee structured investments, in some cases carrying large commissions on top of ongoing asset-based charges. Many were long-duration products. Principal was locked up for years. There was little to no current income for operating needs. Returns materially trailed broad U.S. equity markets.

When the funds tried to sell the structured investments, there were no takers.

The advice

Bruce advised that the holdings were not suitable. Illiquidity meant principal was not available if it was needed. The products did not support operating cash. The economics and performance did not justify the complexity.

The recommendation was to move the accounts to a Registered Investment Adviser organized and operating as a client fiduciary, not a broker, and in general to invest primarily in U.S. Treasuries or Agencies for fixed income and market-beta mutual funds or ETFs for equities.

What followed

The relationship began with investment consulting contracts: first with the district council, then with other ironworker funds. Over time, that work led to a result that served both sides. Leppla Capital was asked to take over investment management for the council fund and many of the related local funds.

That origin still shapes the firm. Clear mandates. Liquid building blocks. Fiduciary duty. Published economics. The same principles guide Layered Cash Management and the Securities Investment Pyramid™ today.
