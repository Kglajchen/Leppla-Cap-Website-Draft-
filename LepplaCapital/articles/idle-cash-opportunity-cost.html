Idle cash

The quiet cost of idle operating cash

Leaving surplus cash in a low-yielding operating account feels safe. Over time, it can still be expensive.

4 min readEducational

Organizations have a duty to protect operating funds. Keeping enough cash for bills is non-negotiable. The harder question is what to do with balances that sit above those needs for long stretches.

Safety has a price. So does inaction.

Operating accounts are built for access. That is their job. When surplus cash sits there for months or years, the organization may forgo income that could have been earned in a more intentional cash structure without giving up the liquidity it actually needs.

Idle is not the same as reserved

A true reserve has a purpose and a horizon. Idle cash is often just leftover balance with no written job. Boards and business managers can fix that by separating operating float from scheduled surplus and from longer-horizon capital.

The goal is not to take unnecessary risk with money that pays bills. The goal is to stop treating every dollar in the operating account as if it has the same job.

What to do next

Map the cash-flow calendar first. Keep the bank as the front door. Then decide what can be scheduled by duration and what, if anything, belongs in a longer reserve account.
