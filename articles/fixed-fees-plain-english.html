Fees

Fixed Fees In Plain English

Published fees and a two-year lock are not marketing slogans. They change how much of the return stays with the organization that owns the capital.

9 min readEducational

Trustees, boards, CFOs, and plan sponsors spend a lot of time on investment return. Far less time is spent on the fee that compounds against that return every quarter. The fee often looks small as a percentage. Over a decade, it is not small in dollars. This guide explains published versus negotiated opacity, what a basis point actually means, how fee drag compounds, what a two-year lock is for, and what any adviser should be able to answer in plain English.

Published Fees Versus Negotiated Opacity

A published fee is a rate the firm states in advance, usually in basis points of assets under management. Anyone can see it. The engagement does not start with a private bargaining session that produces a different number for every client based on relationship leverage or sales pressure.

Negotiated opacity is the opposite pattern. The headline rate may be “about one percent” or “competitive with peers,” while the real all-in cost depends on share class, product wrap, custody, trading spreads, and whether anyone is paid to place the client into a particular vehicle. The board may never see a single clean number. That is not a paperwork inconvenience. It is a governance problem. You cannot oversee what you cannot measure.

For institutional capital, clarity is part of the fiduciary job. A published rate does not magically make a firm honest. It does make the economics auditable. Auditable economics are easier to defend in minutes, RFPs, and board books.

Basis Points Explained Without Jargon

One basis point is one one-hundredth of one percent. In math terms, 1 bp equals 0.01%. So 25 basis points is 0.25% per year. Fifty basis points is 0.50% per year. One hundred twenty-five basis points is 1.25% per year.

On a $5 million account, 25 bp is about $12,500 a year before compounding effects. Fifty bp is about $25,000 a year. One hundred twenty-five bp is about $62,500 a year. Those are simple annual illustrations at a fixed asset level. In a growing account, the dollar fee rises with the balance. That is why the rate matters more than a single-year invoice.

When someone says “only a quarter of a percent,” translate it. Ask for basis points. Ask whether the number is advisory only or all-in. Ask whether product costs sit on top. The board should be able to write one sentence: “We pay X basis points for Y service, and here is what else, if anything, comes out of the portfolio.”

Fee Drag Compounds Against The Client

Gross return is what the portfolio earns before fees. Net return is what remains after fees. Fee drag is the gap. Because fees are taken continuously, they remove capital that would otherwise have stayed invested and compounded.

Consider a hypothetical illustration only. Assume $5 million starting capital, a 12% gross annual return, a 10-year horizon, and quarterly compounding where each quarter’s net growth equals the quarterly gross rate minus the quarterly fee rate. Under those assumed inputs, a 125 bp fee leaves roughly $14.4 million at the end of year ten. A 50 bp fee leaves roughly $15.5 million. The difference is on the order of $1.1 million that stayed with the client instead of leaving as fee drag. Change the return, the years, or the starting capital and the dollars change. The direction does not: a higher ongoing fee compounds against the owner of the capital.

The illustration above is hypothetical, not a forecast or a guarantee. Markets do not deliver a smooth 12% every year. The point is mechanical: on the same assumed gross return, a higher fee leaves less for the fund. See the interactive fee calculator on the Securities Investment Pyramid™ page to vary the inputs yourself.

Boards sometimes treat fees as a line item to renegotiate once and forget. That is a mistake. Fee drag is a permanent haircut on compounding. For pensions, endowments, and operating reserves that must last, permanent haircuts deserve permanent attention.

What A Two-Year Lock Means

A two-year lock means the stated basis-point rate is fixed for a two-year engagement term. The firm does not quietly reprice midstream because markets got harder or because the relationship manager needs a higher payout. The client can budget. The board can document the rate in the mandate and the minutes.

At the end of the two years, the fee restructures to the same published basis points of the then-current account value for the next two-year period. The rate stays published. The dollar amount adjusts with the portfolio size. That is different from an open-ended discretionary schedule that can drift, and different from a teaser rate that rises after the first year.

A lock is about certainty, not about trapping the client in a bad product. The investment structure still has to fit the mandate. The lock simply keeps the pricing transparent for the term you agreed to oversee.

Questions Any Adviser Should Answer

Before you approve an engagement, put these questions in the board packet. Require written answers.

What Is The All-In Fee In Basis Points?

Ask for one number that covers advisory compensation. Then ask what else is paid: commissions, 12b-1 fees, product expense ratios, custody, soft dollars, or placement economics. If the answer is a stack of documents with no summary, that is the answer.

Is The Rate Published And Locked, Or Discretionary?

Published and locked means you can explain it to a successor trustee without a private history lesson. Discretionary means the economics may depend on who asks and when.

What Else Comes Out Of The Portfolio?

Spreads, markups, and embedded product costs can dwarf a headline advisory fee. Ask whether the firm is paid to recommend specific products. Ask whether liquidity and exit costs are disclosed before purchase, not after.

How Does The Fee Relate To The Job Of The Capital?

Operating cash scheduled by duration is a different job from long-horizon reserves. Fees should match the structure and the work, not a one-size wrap around mixed objectives.

What To Do Next

Pull your current advisory, wrap, and product costs into one page. Convert everything you can into basis points. Compare that all-in figure with any published alternative you are considering. Then ask whether the capital’s job (near-term liquidity versus longer reserves) matches the structure you are paying for. If the economics are still hard to summarize in one paragraph, the problem is not your summarization skills.
