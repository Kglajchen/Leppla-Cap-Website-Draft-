Fiduciary Duty

Fiduciary Vs Brokerage

The difference is not cosmetic. It shows up in what gets recommended, what can be exited, and whose interest comes first when incentives collide.

10 min readEducational

Boards hear professional language from every side of the table. “Advice,” “solutions,” “suitable,” “in your interest.” The labels can sound alike. The jobs are not alike. A Registered Investment Adviser acting as a fiduciary is obligated to put the client’s interest first. A brokerage relationship can center on distributing products. Both may employ credentialed people. Only one of those models is built around an ongoing duty of loyalty and care to the organization that owns the capital.

This guide is for trustees, union managers, CFOs, pension committees, and foundation boards who need a working standard, not a seminar. It covers duty in plain terms, product-sale failure modes, what a fiduciary review actually asks, careful language around suitability and best interest, and a practical checklist you can use in the next meeting.

Duty Of Loyalty And Care In Plain Terms

Loyalty

Loyalty means the adviser’s recommendations are for the client, not for the inventory, the commission schedule, or the house product of the quarter. Conflicts must be identified and handled in the client’s favor, not buried in fine print the board will never read. If the firm makes more money when the client buys a more expensive or less liquid product, that conflict is the story, not a footnote.

Care

Care means the work is diligent and informed. The adviser learns the mandate, the cash needs, the constraints, and the time horizon before recommending a structure. Care is not a glossy deck. It is the habit of matching capital to a job the organization actually has.

Together, loyalty and care are why fiduciary advice starts with questions about spending, liquidity, and policy. Product distribution often starts with what is available to sell.

What Goes Wrong In A Product Sale

Product-sale failure modes are familiar to anyone who has cleaned up an institutional portfolio after the fact. Holdings can be hard to understand. Fees can be layered: commissions up front, ongoing asset charges, and product expenses on top. Duration can be long while operating needs are near-term. Liquidity can be thin or contractual. When the organization needs principal, there may be no clean exit at a fair price.

Unsuitability in this setting is practical, not academic. If a fund cannot explain what it owns, cannot access principal when operations require it, and cannot show that the economics justify the complexity, the holding is not doing fiduciary work. Complexity is not sophistication. Complexity is often just opacity with a brochure.

A fiduciary conversation starts with the mandate and the cash. A product conversation often starts with the inventory.

An Institutional Lesson, Without Mythmaking

Leppla Capital’s founding work began in 2017 after Bruce Leppla was introduced to leadership connected with a California ironworkers district council. The concern was straightforward: investment advice to the council fund and related local funds did not look right. The advisers sat in brokerage offices, not fiduciary ones. On review, holdings included unsuitable, illiquid, high-fee structured investments. Some carried large commissions in addition to ongoing asset-based charges. Many locked principal for years, produced little current income for operating needs, and did not justify their complexity.

The advice that followed was equally plain. The holdings were not suitable for the funds’ needs. Illiquidity meant principal was not available if required. The recommendation was to move toward a Registered Investment Adviser organized as a client fiduciary, and in general to favor liquid building blocks such as U.S. Treasuries or Agencies for fixed income and market-beta mutual funds or ETFs for equities. Consulting relationships with the district council and other ironworker funds followed. Over time, Leppla Capital was asked to take on investment management for the council fund and many related local funds.

That origin is not a sales parable. It is a reminder of what boards are for: notice when the structure serves the seller more than the fund, insist on liquidity that matches real cash needs, and prefer clear economics over impressive packaging.

What A Fiduciary Review Asks

A serious review is a set of questions the board can answer in ordinary language. If the answers require a specialist translator, that is already a finding.

Is It Suitable For This Mandate?

Does the holding match liquidity needs, distribution timing, risk limits, and written policy? “It might outperform” is not an answer if the fund cannot live with the path or the lockup.

Is It Understandable?

Can a trustee explain what the organization owns, what drives the return, and how the position is exited? If the board cannot teach it back, oversight is theater.

Are The Economics Clear?

Fees, commissions, product costs, and opportunity cost should be visible in one place. Published basis points beat a scavenger hunt through share-class documents.

Whose Interest Does The Recommendation Serve?

Follow the compensation. If placing the product pays the intermediary more than a simpler alternative that fits the mandate, you are not looking at a neutral menu.

Suitability, Best Interest, And Careful Language

Words in this area are regulated and often used loosely in conversation. Boards should not treat marketing phrases as legal conclusions.

Suitability language generally asks whether a recommendation fits a client’s profile in a transactional setting. That is a real standard in brokerage contexts, and it is not the same thing as an ongoing fiduciary duty to put the client’s interest first across the relationship. “Best interest” phrasing appears in multiple regulatory and marketing contexts. Do not assume the phrase on a slide means the same duty a Registered Investment Adviser fiduciary owes under the Advisers Act framework.

Practical approach for a board: ask what standard the firm accepts in writing, how conflicts are handled, whether the firm is paid to sell products, and whether the engagement is advisory fiduciary or transactional distribution. Then read the Form ADV and the agreement. Labels are cheap. Duties are defined in documents and behavior.

A Practical Standard For Boards

You do not need a philosophy department. You need operating rules.

Keep the bank as the front door for bills and true operating float. Schedule surplus cash by duration so liquidity matches the calendar. Hold longer reserves in a separate account with a written risk framework and liquid building blocks. Prefer published fees over negotiated fog. Require written mandates before broader risk layers. Document why each major holding exists, how it is exited, and what it costs.

When a recommendation fails those tests, the right response is not more complexity. The right response is to ask again whose interest the structure serves.

What To Do Next

At your next committee meeting, put three items on the agenda: (1) a one-page map of who is paid how on the portfolio, (2) a liquidity check on every material holding against the next two years of cash needs, and (3) a plain-language explanation test for the five largest positions. If any item fails, pause new commitments until the failure is fixed.
