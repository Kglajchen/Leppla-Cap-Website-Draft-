Risk Tolerance

Understanding Your Organization's Risk Profile

The right portfolio begins with two honest answers: how much volatility the organization can live with, and when the capital must show up as distributions.

11 min readEducational

Institutional portfolios fail in quiet ways. A committee adopts a risk label that sounds responsible, then funds a structure that cannot support near-term distributions. Or it under-risks long-horizon capital because last year’s drawdown felt personal. Either way, the portfolio stops matching the job of the money.

At Leppla Capital, longer-horizon reserves are organized through the Securities Investment Pyramid™. The build starts with risk tolerance and distribution needs, not with a product list. Broader pyramid layers are added only when a written mandate supports them. This guide walks trustees, boards, CFOs, union managers, and pension committees through the two questions that matter, the seven institutional profiles, and how those profiles map to the pyramid’s layers from Foundational through Direct.

Two Questions That Matter

Risk Tolerance

How does the organization feel about investment risk and swings in portfolio value? Not how a consultant wishes it felt. How it will actually behave when statements are down and stakeholders are asking questions.

  • Low: Prefers stability and is uncomfortable with significant swings, even if that means lower expected long-term returns.
  • Moderate: Can accept some volatility in exchange for higher long-term growth potential, within a written policy.
  • High: Comfortable with substantial fluctuations because the time horizon is long and growth is the priority.

Distribution Needs

When and how much does the organization expect to take from the portfolio? Spending policy, benefit payments, capital calls, and operating support all count. A long “investment horizon” on a slide does not cancel a large distribution next year.

  • Near-term: Material distributions required soon. Principal protection and predictable liquidity take priority.
  • Intermediate: Moderate needs over a multi-year window. Some volatility can be tolerated for growth.
  • Long-term: Limited near-term distributions. Greater flexibility to accept volatility for higher long-term growth potential.

There are no trophy answers. The goal is a profile the board can defend in writing: this risk, this spending path, these layers, this approval process for anything broader.

How Profiles Relate To The Pyramid

Think of the Securities Investment Pyramid™ as a build from the base up. More conservative profiles stay lower in the structure, emphasizing stability and high-quality fixed income. As risk tolerance rises and distribution pressure moves further out, the portfolio can use more of the upper layers. That climb is not automatic and it is not a sales progression. It is mandate-driven.

In practice: answer the two questions, select the profile that fits, then construct the account with only the layers the written policy allows. If leadership later wants broader exposure (sector, thematic, or direct holdings), that is a governance event. Document the approval. Do not improvise it in a market rally.

Near-term operating cash is a different job. Keep the bank as the front door for bills, and use Layered Cash Management when surplus should be scheduled by duration. The pyramid account is for longer-horizon reserves with an explicit risk framework. Mixing those jobs in one undifferentiated pool is how organizations lose track of liquidity.

Seven Institutional Profiles

These profiles combine risk tolerance and distribution horizon. They are planning frames, not promises of return.

1. Very Conservative

Near-term needs, very low risk tolerance. Focus on principal protection: short-term fixed income, cash equivalents, and stable-value style exposures at the base of the pyramid. Upper layers are generally out of scope.

2. Conservative

Near-term needs with limited flexibility, low risk tolerance. High-quality fixed income remains the core, with only a light allocation to broad equity market beta where policy allows.

3. Moderately Conservative

Roughly a 1-5 year distribution window with moderate flexibility. A balanced mix of fixed income and diversified equities, with emphasis on lower-volatility equity exposure rather than concentrated upper-layer bets.

4. Balanced

Roughly a 5-7 year needs profile and moderate risk tolerance. Core diversified equities plus investment-grade fixed income, with room for measured sector exposure when the mandate is clear.

5. Moderately Aggressive

A 5-12 year growth orientation. Growth-oriented equities become central, with meaningful sector and thematic exposure where written approval exists.

6. Aggressive

A 10-20 year horizon and high risk tolerance. Heavy equity allocation, with sector, thematic, and some direct investment opportunities only inside the approved mandate.

7. Very Aggressive

A 15-35 year maximum-growth focus and very high risk tolerance. Maximum equity growth orientation, with substantial thematic, sector, and direct exposure when the board has authorized those layers in writing.

Written Mandate And Approval For Broader Layers

The pyramid’s base is where most institutional portfolios should be able to explain themselves quickly. Foundational and Fixed Income holdings support stability, liquidity, and clarity. Market-beta exposure is often the first deliberate step into equities. Everything above that (asset class tilts, sector funds, thematic lines, direct securities) increases specificity and usually increases the need for oversight.

Broader layers are not “unlocked” by performance envy. They are authorized by policy. Ask, in the minutes: Which layers are permitted today? Who must approve an expansion? What liquidity and concentration limits apply? What is the exit plan if the thesis fails? If those answers are vague, stay lower in the pyramid until governance catches up.

Foundational Through Direct, Briefly

Layers are read from the bottom up. Full walkthroughs live on the Securities Investment Pyramid™ page. The short definitions below are for board literacy.

01 Foundational: The stable core. Broad diversification and capital preservation objectives that anchor the account.

02 Fixed Income: High-quality bonds (primarily U.S. Treasuries, investment-grade corporates, and high-grade municipals) for principal protection, liquidity, and modest income.

03 Market-Beta: Passive market-matching funds or ETFs (for example, broad U.S. equity exposure) that participate in market returns without stock-picking theater.

04 Alpha: The portion of the structure aimed at excess return above passive market returns through active decisions. In pyramid terms, the upper layers are where alpha-seeking work concentrates.

05 Asset Class: Allocations across major equity categories such as large-cap growth, large-cap value, small cap, international, and emerging markets.

06 Sector: Targeted exposure to specific market sectors, typically through sector-focused ETFs or similar vehicles.

07 Thematic: Allocations tied to long-term structural trends (for example, technology infrastructure themes), taken only with eyes open to concentration.

08 Direct: Investments in particular securities rather than through a pooled fund. Highest specificity; highest need for mandate discipline.

Mutual funds and ETFs are pooled vehicles. Mutual funds are typically priced once daily on net asset value. ETFs trade throughout the day. Neither structure replaces the need for a written policy about which layers belong in the account.

Questions For The Next Board Packet

Put the profile choice in writing. Then ask: What distributions are scheduled over the next 24 months? Which pyramid layers are permitted without further approval? Which require a vote? How does this account relate to operating cash at the bank and any layered cash schedule? If the answers disagree with the current holdings, the holdings should change, or the policy should change. Silence is not a third option.

What To Do Next
